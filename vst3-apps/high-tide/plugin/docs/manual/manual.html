HIGH TIDE

A wavetable synth that stores no waveforms. A frame is a bowl on a sculpted terrain, the waveform is what a mass does when it is dropped into it, and position is where the ball is. The tide floods the passes; pins on a timeline tow the ball where you want it. Seventeen starters to play at once, and every control tells you what it is for.

01

The answer and the question

what it is

Every wavetable synth rests on one unquestioned axiom: a frame is one cycle of waveform data, read by a phasor at the pitch. Position, morphing, mip-maps and the stack of frames all follow from it. High Tide keeps the picture and refuses the axiom.

A frame is a bowl. Each column of the terrain is a curve of potential energy. The sound is the trajectory of a mass dropped into that curve, integrated by Newton's law at audio rate. Nothing stores a waveform anywhere in the instrument. A parabola gives a sine, a flat floor with walls gives a triangle in position and a square in velocity, a narrow pit in the floor gives a pulse each time the ball dives through it. You author the cause. The instrument plays the consequence.

The reader is a body, not a phasor. It has energy, so a hard strike climbs higher up the walls and brightens. It has friction, so a note decays by sinking to the floor, where every bowl is locally a parabola, and every note therefore ends as a sine. It has momentum, so nothing snaps.

Position is a place, not a number. The frames stand along a second axis, and the ball moves along it slowly, towed by a tether toward a target. Between two valleys there is a ridge. A low tide leaves the ridge standing and the ball must be dragged over it; a high tide floods the pass and the ball strolls across. Morphing is travel, and the transition between two frames is the shape of the pass, which you can carve.

Pitch is exact by construction

A textbook result says the period of a bowl depends only on its width at each height. Any bowl whose width matches the reference parabola's is exactly isochronous: far from a sine, and in tune whatever the strike. TUNE LOCK in the sculpt rail keeps that rule while you carve. Turn it off and the departure paints the bowl red: that band is the pitch bend the player will hear. The bench measures a tune-locked bowl within 0.01 cents across a 40 dB range of strike.

Nothing to erase. The reference bowl plays a sine with every harmonic below −119 dB, at 110.000 Hz. There is no buffer of samples in the voice for that number to come from. It comes from the physics.
02

Thirty seconds of instructions

first contact

Open the plug-in, or the standalone. It opens on SOFT KEYS, the first of the starters: a warm poly key whose timbre travels a little while you hold it.

Two things to do first. Hover anything at all — a knob, a button, a lane of the timeline — and after a moment a note appears beside it saying what it is and how it is meant to be used. And open FACTORY: the STARTERS group is seventeen ordinary instruments (basses, leads, keys, organs, pads) that behave the way you expect, and the TERRAINS group below it is the instrument showing what else it can do. The HINTS button in the header turns the notes off once you know your way around.

Play a note. The pearl drops into the valley and its swing is drawn as a streak on the terrain. Watch it travel along the position axis as the pin tows it. Hold the note and it stays where the last pin left it; let go and the release pin takes it back.

Move TIDE. The water rises on the relief and the flooded columns rise with it so their floors sit on the water line. That is exactly what the ball feels: a ridge under water is not there. Low tide, and the ridge stands.

Sculpt. Pick PUSH and drag on the terrain. With TUNE LOCK on, the other wall follows and the note does not move. Every brush stroke while a note sounds throws the ball, so every touch is audible.

Pin. Click in the bottom lane of the timeline to add a pin; drag it; right-click it to remove it; double-click it to change its ease. Points left of the red flag count from the key going down; points right of it count from the key coming up. Shift-drag a loop.

Rock. Raise ROCK on a double-well terrain (CASCADE, GROUNDSWELL) and listen to the octave drop in.

03

The terrain and its dictionary

what a shape sounds like

The terrain is a heightmap of 512 samples across the swing axis by 128 columns along the position axis, in height units where the reference bowl is ½x² and a full-scale strike reaches x = ±1. Every feature has one fixed meaning to the ear, and a sculptor learns the vocabulary once.

featuresound
floor of a valleythe note and its quiet timbre; its curvature is the pitch; every decay ends here
wallsthe loud timbre — steep is bright and square-ish, gentle is round
a shelf halfway upa timbre that exists only at mezzo-forte
a pit in the floora pulse: the ball dives through and comes out with a spike in its velocity
two pits with a humpan octave regime that opens when the strike clears the hump
asymmetryeven harmonics; mirror the bowl and they vanish
a ridge along the position axisa morph threshold; its height is the force needed to cross
the pass over a ridgethe sound of the transition — wide and it strolls, a notch and it snaps
a tilt along the position axisthe direction timbre drifts when the playing gets loud
roughnessgrain, down to the brush floor
The terrain view. Contour lines every tenth of a unit, the water at the tide line, the pearl and its streak, the tether, the pins as stakes along the far edge.

The centre line, and why a bowl can be rich and still in tune

Write a bowl as a centre line with the parabola's width around it, x = c(h) ± √(2h). The width at every height is then the reference parabola's whatever c does — so the bowl's centre line may wander with height however it likes and the pitch does not move. A straight centre line leans the bowl and brings in the even harmonics; a wavy one gives a wave nothing like a sine, exactly in tune. That construction is what every starter is built from, and it is what TUNE LOCK maintains while you sculpt.

Three taps

The same motion can be heard three ways. POSITION TAP is where the ball is: the round sound. VELOCITY TAP is how fast it is going: brighter, and where a pit's pulse lives. FORCE TAP is the wall's push on it: the hardest of the three, and it hears a wiggly wall even while the position stays a clean swing. Mix them. TONE is a radiation lowpass after the taps, from 200 Hz to 20 kHz.

The brush floor

The ball cannot see a feature narrower than the distance it travels in one step, and a feature it half-sees is noise. So the brush refuses a radius under 0.04 units, and a fast ball is handed the terrain blurred to about its own step: a high note sees a smoother world, the way a wavetable at a high pitch sees fewer harmonics. The engine also subdivides its step where the terrain is stiff, so a wall bounce is resolved rather than guessed.

04

Sculpting

the left rail

Sculpting happens on the terrain itself. Left-drag paints with the current tool; the brush footprint is a ring under the pointer. Right-drag or alt-drag orbits the camera, the wheel zooms, HOME resets the view. Shift-click drops a ball where you click.

The sculpt rail: tools, brush radius and strength, TUNE LOCK, GENERATE, UNDO and REDO.

Tune lock

With TUNE LOCK on, whatever you do to one wall of a bowl, the other wall is rebuilt so that at every height the width equals the reference parabola's. The pitch cannot move; the shape can do anything. A column with a pit or a shelf is not monotone and the lock skips it, and says so. With the lock off, every column is coloured by how far its width departs from the rule: that red is tension, the pitch bend a strike will produce. It is not a knob; it is something you can see you have carved.

CASCADE with TUNE LOCK off: a double well is nowhere near the width rule, and the whole terrain says so.

Generate, undo, and where terrains come from

GENERATE offers four starting grounds: six valleys with ridges between, one double well with rising ridges, the reference bowl everywhere, and valleys with a little grain. Ctrl-Z and Ctrl-Y undo and redo both terrain and pins. PNG OUT writes the terrain as a 16-bit greyscale PNG and PNG IN reads one back, so any image editor is a terrain editor; PHOTO reads any picture and uses each row's brightness as a bowl profile, the way Photo Synth does.

The ball

the right rail, upper half
controlmeaning
STRIKEthe energy of a full-velocity hit; at 100 % the ball reaches the walls at x = ±1
VELOCITYhow much key velocity scales the strike
FRICTIONenergy lost while the key is down; zero is frictionless and the note holds forever
RELEASEenergy lost once the key is up: the ball sinking to the floor
SERVOzero trusts the bowl's own period; one measures it and corrects the clock each cycle
TONEthe radiation lowpass

The amplitude envelope in the AMP section is an ordinary output VCA, kept for the sake of every DAW. The ball's own energy is the other dynamic: a note under FRICTION 0 and AMP SUSTAIN 100 % rings as long as the key is down, and its timbre closes as the ball sinks. The two are lanes on the timeline, side by side.

The right rail: BALL, TERRAIN, VOICE, AMP, MOD, LFO 1, LFO 2. Every control is a brass-ferruled tide gauge.

Servo and tension

A bowl that breaks the width rule bends its pitch with the strike. A box bowl, for instance, plays a soft note a long way below a hard one, because a ball crawling across a flat floor takes its time. That is a sound, and a good one on a bass. When it is not wanted, SERVO measures the period the bowl actually produced and eases the clock onto the note, one cycle late. The bench holds a box within a few cents at SERVO 70 %.

Unison

UNISON puts up to four balls in the same bowl. DETUNE runs their clocks a little apart — fifty cents across the fan at full — which is the familiar unison spread, and the ball that plays your note is never one of the detuned ones, so the pitch stays exact however wide it gets. SPREAD scatters how hard each ball is struck instead, so they differ in brightness and phase; in an isochronous bowl that is almost no detune, and in a box it is a great deal, because in a box energy is pitch. WIDTH stands them across the stereo field.

06

Pins, the tide and the timeline

the workflow

The bottom of the window is a timeline that runs from the moment a key goes down, per voice. Each lane draws the real curve of a modulator against time: AMP and MOD envelopes as their shapes, LFO 1 and LFO 2 as their waveforms at their rates. Below them three point lanes, TIDE, ROCK and PINS, are yours to draw on.

A pin is a target, not a coordinate. The pin lane defines where the ball is towed at every moment; HOLD is the strength of the tether; the terrain decides how the ball gets there. A ridge in the way slows it or stops it. TIDE floods the relief: everything below the water line is a free sweep, everything above it is a wall. High tide and high hold is the video editor, the ball goes exactly where the pins say. Low tide and low hold is the pure landscape. Everything between is a terrain that partly has its way.

The timeline: envelopes and LFOs drawn as they are, then TIDE, ROCK and PINS. The red flag is the release marker; to its right, time counts from the key coming up.

Two anchors and a loop

Points left of the release marker count from note-on. Points right of it count from note-off, and the release curve starts from wherever the lane was when the key came up, so there is never a jump. A held note stretches the middle, exactly as the envelope does. Shift-drag on a point lane to set a loop: while the key is down, the points inside it cycle. That is how a hand-drawn LFO along the terrain is made. Click the small box at the loop's end to clear it.

Each point carries an ease into it: HOLD steps at the point's time, LINEAR goes straight, SMOOTH eases in and out. Double-click a point to cycle them. An empty TIDE or ROCK lane means the knob rules; a lane with points owns the value for the whole note and the knob is shown as a faint line for reference.

SPRING TIDE: a loop from half a second to a second and a half, cycling the ball between two valleys while the key is held. The coral dots are the wake of the last note.

The wake

The last note's actual path is drawn over the pin lane in coral dots and as a trail on the terrain. Where it follows the pins the tide carried; where it lags or stops, the terrain won. The same key at the same velocity leaves the same wake, to the sample.

High tide: the passes are flooded and the ball is towed anywhere.
Low tide on the same terrain: the ridges stand and the ball is stranded in its valley.
07

Rock and breath

where the nonlinear waveforms live

A ball on a still terrain can never be chaotic; that is a theorem, and it is what makes everything above playable. Add one ingredient and the whole bifurcation literature opens. ROCK tilts the whole terrain like a see-saw, at a ratio of the note. BREATH steepens and relaxes the bowls instead. Both are the same knob, ROCK, under ROCK MODE.

In a double well as ROCK rises: the ball wobbles in one pit, following the drive. Then it takes two rocks to complete one figure, and the pitch drops an exact octave and goes hollow. Then four, then eight, then the cascade closes and the ball hops between the pits at times nobody chose. Inside the chaos there are windows where it settles into a three-rock figure. This is not white noise: the drive stays as a strong line and the disorder is in how the ball disagrees with it. The bench finds the sub-octave at ROCK 30 % on the CASCADE terrain, and proves the chaos as chaos: two strikes a millionth apart diverge to order one within three seconds.

The rock rate is the note, so the pitch survives the chaos. ROCK RATIO offers 1/1, 2/1, 1/2, 1/3, 3/2 and 3/1, or FREE with ROCK RATE in hertz. A rock at the note also pumps an isochronous bowl until friction balances it, so a rocked note sustains like a bowed string and decays down the bowl when the rocking stops. BREATH at 2/1 is the parametric pump, a child on a swing; it has finite power, so it settles at a level rather than growing without end.

Rock is on the timeline too. A ROCK lane that rises over a note walks the sound from a clean tone into period doubling and out the other side, every time the key is pressed, the same way. CASCADE does exactly that over four seconds.

Voices

VOICES is POLY (twelve), MONO (every key restrikes and restarts the timeline, the pitch glides over GLIDE) or LEGATO (an overlapping key only moves the pitch and the timeline runs on). A released key in MONO falls back to the key still held without a new strike. TUNE is twelve semitones either way; the pitch wheel bends two. The mod wheel and channel pressure lift the TIDE.

08

Modulators, patches and the rack

the rest of the machine

The mod envelope and two LFOs

MOD is a second ADSR with a TARGET and a bipolar AMOUNT. Each LFO has a RATE, a SYNC to the host clock (FREE, 4/1 down to 1/16 with triplets), a SHAPE (SINE, TRI, SAW, SQUARE, S&H) and the same TARGET list: POSITION pushes the ball's target along the terrain, TIDE, ROCK, HOLD and TONE offset those controls, PITCH is vibrato. Free LFOs restart at every key so a note is the same note twice; synced ones ride the transport.

Patches

FACTORY lists the twelve built-in terrains. SAVE writes a patch as a JSON file into Documents\Brokild patches\High Tide\, with the terrain inside it as a 16-bit PNG, the pins, every control and the rack. OPEN reads one; PATCHES lists the folder, subfolders as groups. A patch is the whole instrument: terrain, pins, controls and rack together.

The world rack

The teal globe in the header opens the Brokild World FX rack: twelve pedals after the voice sum, the SPECTRA characters that reach inside the voices (their detune is fanned across the balls, their sag is keyed to the gate, their tremolo and filter ride the taps), and five MACROS that are the rack's only host parameters. A fresh rack is bit-identical to no rack at all.
