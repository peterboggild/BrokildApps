High Tide

A wavetable synth that stores no waveforms. Sculpt the terrain; the sound is what falls out of it.

Every wavetable synth keeps a list of numbers and reads it with a pointer. High Tide keeps the landscape those numbers fall out of: a frame is a bowl on a sculpted terrain, and the waveform is a ball dropped into it, integrated by Newton's law at audio rate. A parabola is a sine, a box is a square, a pit in the floor is a pulse. A hard strike climbs the walls and brightens; a note decays by sinking to the floor. Position is where the ball is. A rising tide floods the passes between valleys; pins on a timeline tow the ball exactly where you want it, when you want it, the same way every time. Rock the terrain at the note and the sound period-doubles into chaos, an octave at a time. Seventeen starters to play straight away, and every control on the panel tells you what it is for.

Store the question, not the answer

A frame is a bowl. The waveform is what a mass does in it.

In mechanics a periodic waveform is not data; it is what a ball does when it is dropped into a bowl. Energy is conserved, so at every point of the swing the ball's speed is fixed by how far below the rim it sits. Draw the bowl and you have drawn the wave, from the other side. High Tide hands you the bowl and never the wave. Nothing in the voice stores a sample, and the bench proves it by having nothing to erase: the reference bowl plays a sine with every harmonic below −119 dB.

Steep is bright and square-ish; gentle is round. A shelf halfway up is a sound only forte reaches.

Between two valleys along the position axis. Its height is the force it takes to cross.

In tune by construction

A textbook result says the period of a bowl depends only on its width at each height. TUNE LOCK keeps that rule while you sculpt: shear the walls however you like, the other wall follows, and the pitch does not move with the strike. Turn it off and the departure paints the bowl red; that red is the pitch bend a strike will produce. Tension is not a knob, it is something you can see you carved.

Seventeen sounds to start from, and a panel that explains itself

A new instrument you cannot guess your way into is a curiosity. These two make it playable on the first day.

The FACTORY menu opens in two groups. STARTERS is seventeen ordinary instruments — subs, plucks, wide basses, saw and square leads, an organ, an electric piano, a bell, a clav, pads, a drone — each built on bowls that are exactly in tune whatever the strike, with no drive and a predictable tide. They sound the way their names sound, they are levelled against each other, and they are the place to start editing. TERRAINS below them is the instrument showing what else it is: thresholds, cascades, stranded balls, a parametric pump.

And every control, button, tool and lane of the timeline carries a hint: hover it and a note appears beside it — never under the pointer, never over the control — saying what it is, how it is meant to be used, and what it is set to against its default. One button in the header turns them off when you no longer need them.

Pins, the tide, and a timeline that runs from the key

Keyframes for the wavetable, with the terrain still having a say.

The bottom of the window is a timeline that starts when a key goes down. Envelopes and LFOs are drawn as the curves they actually are. Below them three lanes are yours: TIDE, ROCK and PINS. A pin is a target, never a coordinate: the ball is towed toward it by a tether whose strength is HOLD, and the terrain decides how it gets there. Points left of the red flag count from the key going down; points right of it count from the key coming up. Shift-drag a loop and the pins inside it cycle while the key is held.

TIDE is a water line on the relief. A ridge under water is not there; a ridge above it stands. High tide and high hold is the video editor, the ball goes exactly where the pins say. Low tide and low hold is the pure landscape and the ball is stranded where it was struck. Everything between is a terrain that partly has its way, and the coral wake of the last note shows you where it won.

Rock the terrain

Where the nonlinear waveforms live.

A ball on a still terrain can never be chaotic; that theorem is what makes the sculpting playable. Add one ingredient and the whole bifurcation literature opens. ROCK tilts the terrain like a see-saw at a ratio of the note; BREATH steepens and relaxes the bowls instead. In a double well as the drive rises, the ball takes two rocks to complete one figure and the pitch drops an exact octave; then four; then eight; then it hops between the pits at times nobody chose. The drive stays as a strong line, so the note survives the chaos. Put ROCK on the timeline and every key walks the same road from clean tone into the cascade.

Terrains come from anywhere a heightmap does

Sculpt, stamp, generate, import a PNG, or feed it a photograph.

Ten tools on the left rail: push, pull, smooth, pit, ridge, tilt, mirror, stamp, relief, flatten, with undo and redo. A GENERATE menu offers four starting grounds. PNG OUT writes the terrain as a 16-bit greyscale PNG and PNG IN reads one back, so Photoshop, Blender and World Machine are terrain editors the day it ships. PHOTO reads any picture and uses each row's brightness as a bowl profile. A patch is the whole instrument: terrain, pins, every control and the rack, saved as one file in Documents\Brokild patches\High Tide\.

The world rack

Twelve pedals and six characters, shared by every Brokild synth.

The teal globe in the header opens the Brokild World FX rack: effects on the left that process what the instrument has made, characters on the right that reach back into it. In High Tide the characters' detune is fanned across the unison balls, their sag is keyed to each note's gate, and their tremolo and filter ride the taps. Five MACROS are the rack's only host parameters. An empty rack is not a bypass but an absence: the audio never enters it, to the sample.

Download and install

Two minutes. There is no installer — a VST3 plugin is a folder you copy into place.

  1. Download the zip. High-Tide-VST3-win64.zip.
  2. Unzip it. Right-click → Extract All. Inside you get a High Tide.vst3 folder, a standalone High Tide.exe, the manual and a readme.
  3. Copy the plugin folder into the VST3 directory. Move the whole High Tide.vst3 folder — not just the file inside it — into C:\Program Files\Common Files\VST3\. Windows will ask for administrator permission; that is normal.
  4. Rescan plugins in your DAW. In Ableton Live: Preferences → Plug-Ins → Rescan. It appears as an instrument called High Tide, by Brokild. Put it on a MIDI track and play.
If the plugin window is blank, install the Microsoft Edge WebView2 runtime. The interface is drawn with WebView2, which ships with current Windows but can be missing on older or heavily stripped installations. The audio keeps working with the window shut either way.
If Windows refuses to load it at all and mentions an Application Control policy, that is Smart App Control. It decides per file, from a cloud reputation lookup, and it can block one unsigned binary while loading another quite happily. The block attaches to that exact copy of the file rather than to the plugin. Windows Security → App & browser control → Smart App Control lets you turn it off — note that switching it off is not reversible without reinstalling Windows. Most machines do not have it enabled at all.
