SNARE TACTICS

A head, a set of wires and a stick. Every snare there is, from those three.

A studio snare, an 808 and a gabber snare are not three different machines. They are the same three things set differently: a drum head, the steel wires under it, and whatever hits it. Snare Tactics models those — including the part drum machines leave out, that the wires are driven by the head — and puts a gated room, a dub echo, four drives and a compressor after them. Studio snares, the classic machines, techno, dub, industrial and digital hardcore, from thirty-one controls. No samples.

Three sources, coupled

A drum machine’s snare is two tones and some filtered noise, each minding its own business. A real snare is a system.

The head is a membrane: its overtones sit at the Bessel-zero ratios, and where you hit it decides which ones speak — a centre hit cannot excite the off-centre modes at all, the edge brings them in, the rim rings the shell. SKIN walks from the 808/909’s two oscillators to that membrane; STRIKE walks from the centre to a rim shot; RING is the gel you put on it, or don’t.

The wires are where synthesised snares usually give up. Real wires are shaken by the head, so they start a fraction of a millisecond after the stick, slap against the head once a cycle when they are loose — a buzz at the drum’s own pitch — and keep sizzling for as long as the head rings. TENSION does all of that, from a loose, dark garage rattle to a tight, bright crack. Play softly and the balance tips toward the wires, the way ghost notes do.

The display renders the hit twice every time a control moves: the head alone in orange, the whole snare in steel behind it. Whatever is steel and not orange is the wires, the stick, the clap and the room.

The four articulations

Snare, rim shot, cross-stick and clap on the GM notes 38, 40, 37 and 39 — a studio snare, then a 909.

The controls

Thirty-one, in nine sections, left to right in the order the sound meets them.

Head

The drum itself

TUNE 100–420 Hz, with the note name. DROP and BEND: how far the pitch starts above it and how fast it falls. DECAY to −60 dB.

WAVE sine, triangle (909), square. SKIN from two oscillators to a membrane. RING damped to wide open. BODY its level.

Wires

The snare in the snare drum

WIRES their level — the 808’s SNAPPY. SIZZLE their decay. TENSION loose (dark, late, buzzing at the head’s pitch, ringing along) to tight (bright, short, crisp). AIR the top end.

Hit

Where, and with what

STRIKE centre, edge, rim shot. STICK the click. CLAP an 808-style clap layered on top, SPREAD its burst spacing, from one tight burst to four hands.

Era

The machine and the room

GRIT sample rate and bit depth: 12-bit SP-1200 and Akai near 30 %, 8-bit Linn near 55 %. ROOM a room behind the drum. GATE the 80s: keyed by the hit, so it closes exactly on time.

Echo

The dub

ECHO a ping-pong tape echo, each repeat darker and thinner, send and feedback on one knob. TIME 1/16 to 1/2, locked to the host’s tempo. The only thing on the panel that makes it stereo.

Transient

Crack and tail

ATTACK and SUSTAIN, bipolar. The shaper reads the synth’s own envelopes — head, wires, stick, clap — so its gain cannot ripple.

Drive

Four engines

From Battlestar Overdrive: IDLE BURN, HYPERDRIVE, RAZOR WING, SUPERNOVA. DRIVE and COLOUR. Level-matched, with anti-aliasing: −88 dB at full RAZOR WING.

Comp & Out

Crack or pump; touch and keys

COMP and SPEED, 1.5 ms look-ahead. LEVEL under a soft ceiling, VELO, and KEYS: FIXED, GM KIT or CHROMATIC.

All four come from the same controls, so a patch is a whole snare rather than one sound, and a drum rack that sends the General MIDI notes just works. KEYS set to FIXED makes every note the snare; CHROMATIC lets the note tune the head.
