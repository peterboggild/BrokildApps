A Brokild polysynth

Owner's handbook

BUILD 260923.1

Eight voices of two ranks each. Sync, poly-mod, a ring modulator and unison. A formant choir behind a three-phase ensemble. Then everything goes to tape before it goes to the hall. September 2026.

What it is

Eight voices, sixteen ranks,
one tape

Installing

Copy 1984.vst3 into your VST3 folder — normally C:\PROGRAM FILES\COMMON FILES\VST3 — and rescan. The standalone in the same download needs nothing installing.

A voice in 1984 is not one synthesizer. It is two, called rank I and rank II, sharing a keyboard, a ring modulator and a sub-oscillator. Each rank has its own oscillator, its own resonant high-pass into its own resonant low-pass, its own filter envelope, its own amplifier envelope, and its own level and pan. Eight voices is therefore sixteen complete ranks, and every patch on the dial is two instruments played as one.

The architecture is the one the big polysynth of 1977 used and nobody has really used since: two ranks per voice, a high-pass and a low-pass in series, and a filter envelope with an initial level and an attack level instead of a sustain. The strange part — hard sync, poly-mod, unison — comes from the other side of the Atlantic. The choir is a fixed formant bank behind a three-phase ensemble.

The eight voices are summed and go through one chain: drive, ensemble, choir, tape, hall, and then the Brokild World FX rack. Everything up to and including the tape runs oversampled, so nothing that saturates can alias. The hall runs at the base rate, because it is linear and long.

The first minute

Dial up CS STRINGS and hold a chord. Then take UNISON DETUNE up, put the voice mode on DUO, and listen to eight oscillator boards instead of four. Everything else in this handbook is detail.

The mechanism

The signal path

Solid lines are audio, broken red lines are modulation, dotted teal lines are envelopes. One filter envelope drives both filters of its rank, and that is the thing that makes this architecture sound the way it does: the high-pass and the low-pass move together, so the note changes shape from both ends at once.

Rank II's wrap resets rank I's phase when SYNC is on: the audible rank is the slave, the way the Prophet does it. Rank II's output, taken before its filter, can bend rank I's pitch, its pulse width and both low-pass cutoffs — so rank II can be an audible layer, a silent modulator, or both at once.

Everything up to and including the tape runs oversampled, so nothing that saturates can fold back into the band. The hall runs at the base rate, because it is linear and long and gains nothing from the extra work. The whole voice is drawn once here; there are eight of them.

The signal path03

The rank strip, 1 of 3

The oscillator

One phase accumulator per rank, four band-limited waves out of it at once, each with its own slider. They are not a selector: saw plus a little pulse plus a little sine is one sound, and it is the brass recipe.

Saw · Pulse · Triangle · Sine

four levels, not a switch

Mix them. The saw is the body, the pulse the reed, the triangle the flute, and the sine weight underneath. The sine is taken after the filter, so it survives when everything else has been shut away.

Width · PWM

50 to 95 per cent

WIDTH sets the pulse; PWM hands it to the sub-oscillator. A slow PWM on two ranks is most of a string patch.

Noise

white, per voice, into the filter

It goes in ahead of the high-pass, so the filters shape it exactly as they shape the oscillator.

Feet · Semitone · Fine

32' 16' 8' 4' 2' · ±12 semitones · ±50 cents

Nearly every factory patch puts rank II a handful of cents off rank I and leaves it there: that beat is the sound of two oscillator boards.

Sync

SYNC I TO II resets rank I's phase every time rank II wraps, band-limited at the actual jump height — so a synced rank is a hard edge and not a burst of aliasing. Rank II is the master because the envelope's pitch route lands on rank I: turn rank II's level down, and the envelope sweeps the synced, audible rank over a note the silent master holds.

Measured

Both ranks sit within one cent at every footage. A saw at A6 keeps its worst non-harmonic component below −60 dB at 2x.

The oscillator04

The rank strip, 2 of 3

Two filters, one envelope

HPF · HPF Res

10 Hz to 12 kHz, 12 dB per octave

A resonant high-pass ahead of the low-pass. This is what makes a string patch thin and a choir patch hollow, and it is the half of the architecture that most synthesizers do not have. Push the resonance and it will sing; the band-pass state is softly limited so a screaming resonance stays a note instead of a spike.

LPF · LPF Res

20 Hz to 20 kHz

The main filter. Resonance runs to self-oscillation in both circuits, and the last few per cent of the sweep are where it crosses over into oscillating on its own.

Filter

two circuits, per rank

CS 12 dBA state-variable pair. Open, glassy, and the one that lets the high-pass be heard.
Ladder 24 dBFour poles with a soft saturator at the summing node. Thicker, and it takes a bass line down where a 12 dB filter cannot.

The two ranks can run different circuits. A ladder rank under a CS rank is most of what the brass patches are doing.

What sets a cutoff

CUTOFF = slider x 2^(env + key + brilliance
    + velocity + touch + sub + poly-mod)

Everything that opens a filter does it in octaves and they add. The filter envelope alone is worth up to six of them.

Brilliance · Resonance

global, bipolar, on both ranks

Two performance trims that ride over whatever the patch set: BRILLIANCE shifts both cutoffs, RESONANCE scales both resonances. They read from −100 to +100 per cent, with the centre meaning "as the patch has it".

Key Track

how far the cutoff follows the note

At zero the filter stays put and the top of the keyboard goes dull; at full it follows the note and the timbre is even everywhere.

Measured

Asked to self-oscillate at 1 kHz, the state-variable filter sits at 1000.0 Hz and the ladder at 996.5 Hz — 0.35 per cent — and both stay bounded. Resonance at 80 per cent puts the cutoff 14.5 dB over the octave above it.

The filters05

The rank strip, 3 of 3

The envelopes, and the end of the rank

Initial Level · Attack Level

both bipolar, −100 to +100 per cent

The filter envelope has no sustain, because IL and AL are the sustain. At key down it jumps to IL, moves to AL over the attack time, then decays to zero. Both are bipolar, so a filter can open from below (IL negative, AL positive) or slam shut from above.

VCF Attack · Decay · Release

1 ms to 10 s · 2 ms to 20 s

Release runs from wherever the envelope had got to when you let go, not from a fixed point — so a short note releases from high up and a held one from near zero.

VCA Attack · Decay · Sustain · Release

the ordinary one

An RC-shaped ADSR that retriggers from its current level, so a repeated note does not click. Sustain at 100 with a long decay is an organ; sustain at 0 with a short decay is a bell.

Level · Pan · Velocity · Vel>Brill

the end of the strip

LEVEL is the rank in the voice — set rank II to zero and it is a silent modulator. PAN places it; the global STEREO SPREAD widens the eight voices around it. VELOCITY is how hard you have to play for full level, and VEL>BRILL hands velocity to the filter instead.

The envelopes06

Performance

Touch, wheel and the sub-oscillator

Touch

aftertouch, channel or polyphonic

Lean into a held key and the note answers. Four destinations, each with its own depth: TOUCH>PITCH, TOUCH>BRILL, TOUCH>LEVEL and TOUCH>SUB, which brings in vibrato only when you press. If the keyboard sends both channel and polyphonic pressure, the higher of the two wins for that note.

Wheel

vibrato, brilliance, rate

WHEEL>VIBRATO and WHEEL>BRILL with a dedicated WHEEL RATE from 2 to 10 Hz, so the wheel's vibrato is its own speed and does not disturb whatever the sub-oscillator is already doing.

The sub-oscillator

one per voice · 0.05 to 40 Hz · seven shapes

Sine, triangle, saw, ramp, square, sample-and-hold and smooth noise, with a delay from 10 ms to 5 s so vibrato arrives after the note has settled. Four destinations:

Sub>Pitchvibrato
Sub>WidthPWM — the string sound
Sub>Filtera growl, or a very slow tide
Sub>Leveltremolo

Sub phase — the one that matters

FreeEvery voice runs its own. Eight voices wandering independently is a section of players.
ResetFrom zero at each note-on. Tight, deliberate, good for a lead.
OneAll eight share a phase. A chord modulated in lockstep is a synthesizer on a tape.

This is one switch and it changes what the instrument is. Try the same pad on FREE and on ONE.

The strange things

Ring modulator & poly-mod

Ring — three modes

OffWhere it sits on a fresh instrument.
CarrierA sine carrier of its own, 0.1 Hz to 4 kHz. Slow it down and it is tremolo; speed it up and it is metal.
RanksRank I multiplied by rank II. Tune them apart and you get sums and differences that have nothing to do with the key.

Key · Speed · Depth

the carrier

With KEY off, SPEED is a fixed frequency in hertz and the ring is inharmonic everywhere on the keyboard — which is exactly what a bell wants. With KEY on, SPEED becomes a ratio of the note, from a quarter to eight times, and the modulation tracks the key. DEPTH crossfades from dry to fully ring-modulated.

Attack · Decay · Mod

the carrier has an envelope of its own

MOD is bipolar: positive sweeps the carrier up over the note, negative sweeps it down. A downward sweep with a long decay is a struck bar; an upward one is the sound of a machine starting.

Poly-mod

two sources, five destinations

Modulation inside the voice, at audio rate, per voice — so eight notes each modulate themselves rather than sharing one LFO. There are two sources. One is rank II, taken before its filter; the other is rank I's own filter envelope.

II>I Pitchexponential FM at audio rate. A little is a growl; a lot is a klaxon.
II>I Widthrank II opens and shuts rank I's pulse, cycle by cycle.
II>Filterfilter FM into both low-passes.
Env>I Pitchbipolar. The classic sweep: the envelope bends the note.
Env>I Widthbipolar. The pulse opens as the filter does.

Set rank II's level to zero

Poly-mod takes rank II from before its amplifier, so its LEVEL slider decides only whether you also hear it. At zero it is a pure modulator and rank I is the whole sound. Leave it up and it is both at once. POLY-MOD SWEEP on the patch dial is the first case; go and look at it.

The instrument as a whole

Voice modes, glide, vintage

Voice mode

PolyEight voices, one per key. A ninth note steals the oldest.
DuoTwo voices per key, detuned and panned apart: four-note polyphony, but every note is a pair of players.
UnisonAll eight on one note, fanned symmetrically around it by UNISON DETUNE, up to 60 cents.
MonoLast note wins, with legato if you want it.

DUO and UNISON both read UNISON DETUNE. It is the single most effective control on the panel for making an eight-voice machine sound like a wall.

Glide · Glissando · Legato

5 ms to 5 s, or OFF

GLISSANDO makes the slide step through semitones instead of sweeping. LEGATO only glides when you are already holding a note, so a phrase slides and a fresh attack does not.

Vintage

one knob, five tolerances

At zero the eight voices are identical, which no analogue polysynth ever was. Turn it up and each voice gets its own oscillator tuning error, its own cutoff offset, its own envelope times, a slow wandering drift and a fast jitter — separately per rank. A poly patch stops being one synthesizer played eight times and becomes eight slightly different synthesizers.

Most factory patches sit between 40 and 55 per cent. Very high is a machine that needs servicing, which is occasionally what you want.

Master tune · Fine · Bend range

±12 semitones · ±50 cents · 0 to 24

These four, with VOLUME and QUALITY, are the only controls a factory patch does not touch — load a patch and your tuning, your bend range and your output level stay where you put them.

Quality

1X / 2X / 4X. Everything up to and including the tape runs at this rate. 2X is the default and what the patches were made on. Eight voices with every stage running measure 2.5 per cent of one core at 1x, 4.6 at 2x and 10.4 at 4x. It is not automatable, and it is not saved into a patch.

Drive

before the decimator, so it cannot alias

OffWhere a fresh instrument sits.
ValveAsymmetric, biased, and therefore even harmonics. Warmth rather than dirt.
TapeA soft knee, symmetric. Thickens without changing the shape much.
FuzzHard, with a tilt ahead of it. For leads that have to cut.
FoldA sine folder. Past a point it stops being distortion and becomes a different instrument.

DRIVE TONE is a tilt afterwards, bipolar. Each mode carries a fixed trim measured on the bench, so turning DRIVE up changes what it sounds like rather than simply making it louder.

Ensemble

three taps per channel, two LFOs, 120 degrees apart

Chorus ITwo taps, the slow LFO only. Gentle.
Chorus IITwo taps, the fast wobble. Closer to a flutter than a chorus.
EnsembleThree taps, both LFOs. The string machine.
CathedralThree taps, both LFOs, deeper, the wet path darkened as a bucket brigade darkens it.

Choir

five formants, U → O → A → E → I

A fixed bank of five band-passes at the formants of a vowel. VOWEL slides continuously through all five — it is a position, not a selector, and everything between two vowels exists. REGISTER shifts the whole bank up to 40 per cent either way, which is the difference between a man and a boy. AIR adds breath on top. MIX is how much of it you want.

Feed it a pulse-rich rank and it is a human voice. Put the ensemble behind it and it is a choir.

Measured

Three modulated taps partly cancel — −4.3 dB at full mix — so the wet path is normalised between the amplitude sum and the power sum rather than by either.

Measured

WOW at full and 0.5 Hz moves the pitch 2.15 per cent peak to peak; the sine alone would give 1.1 and the walk adds the rest. FLUTTER at full: 0.35. Both at zero: 0.0003 — nothing. HISS and AGE at full: −57 dBFS in the second after a note, and exactly silent when nothing plays.

A 2 s DECAY measures 1.68 s. At maximum decay, size, shimmer and motion the tail is 97 dB lower forty seconds after the note than in its first seconds: bounded, and falling.

The factory patches13

Keeping your own

Patches on disk

The forty on the dial are read-only. Everything you make goes to disk as plain JSON, in the house folder every Brokild instrument uses:

DOCUMENTS \ BROKILD PATCHES \ 1984

SAVE and OPEN are ordinary file dialogs, so you can keep patches in folders or send one to somebody; the browser beside them lists the house folder, subfolders and all, without a dialog. A patch file carries the whole instrument: all 126 parameters, and the Brokild World FX rack blob inside it, so a patch that was made with an echo and a room in the rack comes back with them. The performance controls do not travel — master tune, fine, bend range, volume and quality stay as you have them when a patch loads.

Random

RANDOM rolls a whole instrument, not a spray of values: the oscillators land on shapes that go together, the filters inside musical ranges, the envelopes in usable times, and the master and the performance controls are left alone.

What the numbers mean

Frequencies and times run on an exponential sweep, so a slider is fine where it needs to be: a cutoff covers 20 Hz to 20 kHz, an attack 1 ms to 10 s. A bipolar control reads −100 to +100 per cent about a centre of zero, tuning is in cents, width 50 to 95 per cent, and a glide under one per cent reads OFF.

The world rack

Brokild World FX, and the five macros

Two rooms in a row

1984 has a hall of its own and the rack has a SPACE. Running both is legitimate and occasionally wonderful, but the usual answer is to pick one: the instrument's hall for the sound of the machine, the rack's for the sound of the record.

Every Brokild instrument carries the same rack at the end of its chain, and 1984 is no exception. Open it with the globe and you get a pedalboard: a column of effect modules you can switch on, reorder and set, and a second column of SPECTRA characters that reach into the instrument itself rather than filtering its output. A fresh instrument has an empty rack and sounds exactly as though the rack were not there; a patch you save carries whatever you built in it. It is the same rack, with the same modules and the same presets, as the one in every other machine on the shelf, so a chain you like moves with you.

BWFX MACRO 1 to 5 are the rack's only host parameters, and they are the reason it can be automated at all. A macro maps a control in the rack: assign one and it takes that control from the bottom of its range to the top as the macro sweeps from 0 to 100 per cent, or the other way round with a negative depth, and the control visibly moves as it does. One destination belongs to one macro. Macro 5 arrives assigned to the rack's own dry/wet at full negative depth, so a fresh instrument with the macro at rest is exactly the sound it always was, and pushing it up is one global effects fader.

Where the rack sits

After the hall and before the output. The instrument's own drive, ensemble, choir, tape and hall are not part of it and do not duplicate it — they are the machine, and the rack is what you put the machine through.

What the bench proves17

Credits

1984

The CS-80, the Prophet-5 and the VP-330 are the machines it is after, and it copies none of them.

What it takes from each is the idea, not the circuit. From the first: two ranks per voice, a high-pass in series with a low-pass, and a filter envelope built out of an initial level and an attack level instead of a sustain. From the second: hard sync, poly-mod and unison, and the habit of letting an oscillator modulate a filter at audio rate. From the third: a fixed formant bank behind a three-phase ensemble, which is how a machine can sound like people.

Everything in it was written from scratch, measured on a bench, and kept only if the measurement agreed with the claim. Nothing was tuned by ear afterwards and then described as physics.

Where to start

CS Strings for what the architecture is for. Vangelis Choir for the choir and the hall. Poly-Mod Sweep for the strange half. Naked Rank I for one oscillator with nothing on it at all, which is where to go when you want to build something yourself.
