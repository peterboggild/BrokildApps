Blade Ruiner

Three synthesisers, one city, and at least one of them always awake.

An atmospheric synthesiser in three layers, each with its own engine and its own room. LOS ANGELES is a drone that does not stop. DECKARD is the polysynth you play over it. REPLICANT is the machine in the next room that has been counting all along. One dial marked MOOD, running 0 to 999, is the whole patch. Any combination of the three can run at once — but not none of them, because an atmosphere that can be silenced by one click is a mixer channel.

Three engines, not three presets

They share an output stage and a keyboard. Everything else about them is different, including what they look like.

Layer one

Los Angeles

Nine oscillators on one chord, a rain of filtered noise, grains of dust, and a reverb big enough to lose a city in. It does not need a keyboard.

Layer two

Deckard

Eight voices, two oscillators and a sub each, a four-pole ladder with its own envelope, a vibrato that arrives late, and the ensemble chorus that does the rest.

Layer three

Replicant

Sixteen steps on the host’s clock, built from a six-bit seed, driving a two-operator FM voice with an inharmonic ratio and a heartbeat on the downbeat.

The city

Load the plugin and it is already sounding. LOS ANGELES is a drone, and the page it lives on is lit by what the instrument is doing.

ControlWhat it does
VOICINGWhich chord the nine oscillators spread across. TRITONE is the one that makes the city feel wrong.
SPRAWLHow far apart they are detuned. Small values beat slowly; large ones stop being a chord and become a place.
SMOGHow dark. The master control of the whole layer.
KIPPLEEntropy: an asymmetric saturation, a slowly-moving comb, and grains of dust at random pitches. Philip K. Dick’s word for the useless matter that accumulates in an empty building whether or not anybody adds to it.
NEONShimmer — an octave-up copy of the reverb tail folded back into its own input, so the top of the sound keeps climbing.
DRIFTEvery oscillator has its own drift at its own speed, between forty seconds and two minutes a cycle. Slow enough that you never catch it.

The one you play

DECKARD is built the way the big Japanese polysynth of 1977 was built, and it is at its best held rather than played.

Two oscillators and a sub per voice with SAW, PULSE, SAW+PULSE and RING shapes; a four-pole ladder whose resonance runs through a saturator, which is what makes a ladder sound like a ladder rather than like four filters; a filter envelope scaled by velocity; a vibrato that arrives over about a second of held note and stops when you let go; and an ensemble chorus of three modulated taps a third of a cycle apart. Somewhere around 70 % the ensemble stops being a chorus and becomes the sound.

Sixty-four machines

REPLICANT’s whole pattern is a six-bit number. Same seed, same machine — in this session, in this project, and on anybody else’s computer.

METAL walks the modulator up a ladder of ratios a bell has and a piano does not — 1, 2, √2, 3, and on up to 7.07 — so at the bottom it is a soft mallet and at the top there is no fundamental you could name. GLITCH is sample-and-hold plus bit reduction, and above about 45 % it starts occasionally stuttering; which steps stutter is decided fresh each time, so it never becomes part of the pattern. MENACE is drive, resonance and the sub heartbeat that lands on step one.

All sixty-four are audibly different. The bench renders every seed and fingerprints its first bar; no two of the 2,016 pairs collide.

A thousand moods, and every one of them is a patch

There is a dial in the header marked MOOD. It goes from 0 to 999, and each of those thousand numbers is an entire instrument with its own line of text.

Dialling a number writes every character parameter in all three layers — which are live, the voicing, the weather, the oscillator, the sequence seed, the shape of everything — and prints what the number means. RANDOM simply dials at random. Levels, tuning and the limiter are never touched, so a mood changes what the machine is thinking about and not how loud it is.

Every mood makes a sound on its own, and the dial reads — until you turn it. The patch the plugin ships with is hand-made and is not one of the thousand, so printing a number over it would be a small lie. And “one layer live” is not enough to guarantee a sound — Deckard needs keys — so if nothing would be free-running, the city comes on and stays on.
The same number is always the same instrument. Nothing about a mood depends on the clock, the session or the machine: 481 today is 481 next year and 481 on somebody else’s computer. That is the only thing that makes a three-digit number worth writing down.

Nineteen of the descriptions are written by hand, and five of those are Philip K. Dick’s own — 3, 382, 481, 594 and 888, from Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? The dial marks the written ones as you pass them. The other 981 come from the seed Two of the nineteen are not Dick at all: 451 and 42 belong to other books by other authors, and it would be a poor sort of dystopia that did not nod to its neighbours. Neither of them winks. The rest are generated from the seed by a small grammar of nine sentence shapes, each with its own vocabulary chosen so that every combination inside it is a sentence somebody might actually write.

No two of the thousand share a line. Drawing the words at random gave 664 distinct descriptions out of 1000, so the mapping is injective by construction instead — and the bench checks all thousand, both text and patch, on every build.

Download and install

Download and install

Two minutes. There is no installer — a VST3 plugin is a folder you copy into place.

  1. Download the zip. Blade-Ruiner-VST3-win64.zip (6.9 MB).
  2. Unzip it. Right-click → Extract All. Inside you get a Blade Ruiner.vst3 folder, a standalone Blade Ruiner.exe, the manual and a readme.
  3. Copy the plugin folder into the VST3 directory. Move the whole Blade Ruiner.vst3 folder — not just the file inside it — into C:\Program Files\Common Files\VST3\.
  4. Rescan plugins in your DAW. In Ableton Live: Preferences → Plug-Ins → Rescan. It appears as an instrument called Blade Ruiner, by Brokild. Put it on a MIDI track and hold a chord.
If the plugin window is blank, install the Microsoft Edge WebView2 runtime. The interface is drawn with WebView2, which ships with current Windows but can be missing on older or heavily stripped installations. The audio keeps working with the window shut either way.
If Windows refuses to load it at all and mentions an Application Control policy, that is Smart App Control. It decides per file, from a cloud reputation lookup, and it can block one unsigned binary while loading another quite happily — build 260822.1 of this plugin was blocked on the machine it was written on while three other unsigned plugins in the same folder loaded, and rebuilding the identical program cleared it. The block attaches to that exact copy of the file rather than to the plugin. Failing that, Windows Security → App & browser control → Smart App Control lets you turn it off — note that switching it off is not reversible without reinstalling Windows. Most machines do not have it enabled at all.

Blade Ruiner — a native VST3 instrument. Native C++ audio, JUCE 8, interface drawn in the plugin’s own window. Source and issues: github.com/peterboggild/BrokildApps. Its stablemates are The Mars Wars, Photo-Synth 2 and Escape Room.
