How it works

Playing it

Photo 1 is the oscillator: drag a finger over it and the colour under the fingertip becomes a tone. Photo 2 is the filter: touch it and the colour there shapes that tone. Both pads work at the same time — one finger on each.

Latch leaves the cursor where you lifted your finger. On the tone pad the note keeps sounding; on the filter pad the filter setting is held. Tap again to move the cursor, or turn latch off to release.

Play · Edit · per-pad Rec

Play shows only the photos — everything is played by touch. Edit opens all the controls: sound, effects, motion, files and this text. Each photo has its own Rec button: arm only the pad you want, then draw with one finger or use two fingers to define a line or circle. The saved motion begins looping as soon as you lift. A tap restarts its phase; a drag takes manual control without erasing it. With latch on it keeps running on its own.

Envelope (photo 3)

The third photo shapes each note the way an ADSR does: red sets the attack (more red, snappier — from a 1.5 s swell down to 2 ms), green sets the sustain level (how loud a held note stays), and blue sets the decay and release (from 30 ms up to a 4 s tail). Untouched, the sliders under Sound rule. It is latchable and automatable like the others. Two sliders under Sound choose what the envelope acts on: Env → Amp is how much it shapes loudness (below 100 % the level blends toward a plain click-free gate), and Env → Filter sweeps the filter cut-off with the envelope — up to five octaves at full, closing when the envelope is down and landing on the filter setting at its peak (analog engine). Legato keeps the envelope alive for a moment after you lift, so tapping from colour to colour glides instead of retriggering every note.

FX photo (pad 4)

The fourth pad is a photo like the others — camera, camera roll, demo, live feed, latch and motion recording all work on it. The colour under the cursor drives three effect parameters, chosen with the pull-down menus under the pad: the red channel's strength sets the first, green the second, blue the third. Black means all three off, pure white means all three at 100 %, and everything in between blends. The values are written straight into the sliders in Edit, so lifting your finger keeps them where you left them; Reset puts the three assigned sliders back to their defaults. Note that a parameter only sounds if its effect module is switched on in the Effects panel.

Voices — Mono/Unison and Polyphonic

Voice mode (Sound) chooses how the sixteen-voice pool is used. In Mono/Unison, everything plays one note and the Unison voices slider (1–12) stacks extra voices that sample the colours in rings around your finger — the radius set by Voice spread — so the local texture of the photo becomes a chord riding on that one note. Uniform areas thicken into a unison; detailed areas scatter into intervals.

In Polyphonic mode you can hold up to 4 notes at once — chords on the on-screen keyboard, the computer keys, or in a MIDI file all sound together (oldest note is stolen when you exceed the count). Each note carries 1–4 oscillators: the first plays the colour under your finger, the rest sample a small ring around it, so every note in the chord still gets the photo's texture. Touching the tone pad plays a note at the base note. Hold sustains released keys until it is switched off.

Stereo width pans voices by sampled hue or horizontal cursor position and adds a small per-voice tuning offset; at zero, left and right are identical for true mono compatibility. Both modes need the analog engine, and idle voices cost nothing — only the voices actually sounding burn CPU.

Effects

A Skin switch at the top of the panel chooses between the uniform Rack faceplates and Boutique, where every unit wears its own enclosure — hot-rod red tube, sunburst phaser, dreamy blue chorus, glitch-violet stutter, cassette-label lofi, tape-echo green delay and a deep-space reverb. Purely cosmetic and remembered on the device.

The Effects panel is a real serial signal chain of seven modules: the tube stage, a vintage phaser (four swept all-pass stages with feedback), a vintage chorus (two opposite-phase modulated delay lines, rolled off dark), a rhythmic stutter gate, a lofi stage (sample-rate crush, bit grit, hiss and crackle, soft dirt — all smoothed, analog engine), the two-channel delay and the convolution reverb with Room, Hall, Plate and Spring characters — spring is a chain of dispersive chirp echoes, the classic boing. Drag the handle to reorder; tap a module to expand its controls. Each module has a ⏻ power switch: a switched-off module is removed from the audio graph entirely, so bypassing genuinely reduces CPU load — phaser, chorus, stutter and lofi start switched off, and switching happens under a 60 ms master dip so it never pops. The delay's Character switch chooses clean digital repeats or a tape mode (saturating, dark, wobbly loop), and Offset L↔R delays one side up to 250 ms after the other for wide, staggered echoes. The order and switches are remembered on this device, every module preserves the stereo image, and the tube stage is loudness-compensated.

Spectrum

The Spectrum panel, under Effects, holds characters rather than effects. A Spectrum module is not a box in the signal path — it changes how every voice is built in the first place, which is why each one wears its own enclosure instead of a shared faceplate. Arming one re-colours the whole instrument; disarmed it costs nothing at all. Dark Drone is the first of them.

Dark Drone

One switch (Spectrum → Dark Drone) arms a drone engine, and none of it costs any CPU while the switch is off. Sub level / Sub octave put a thickened sine one or two octaves under every voice, through the same filter. Cluster replaces the tidy ±3-cent unison with an irregular microtonal spread of up to ±60 cents per voice step. Env → Pitch makes notes sag toward an octave down as their envelope decays. Drift is an ultra-slow random walk (30 s to 5 min characteristic time) leaning on the cut-off, the detune and the delay time, so a held drone slowly comes apart on its own. Two live gestures sit in the top bar: Freeze holds the delay tail forever — the loop's input closes, feedback goes to unity under a soft limiter and the damping opens wide — and Halt is a tape-stop that spins the pitch down (press again to spin back up). The delay's feedback now reaches 112 %: past unity the loop self-oscillates into a tanh limiter, and in tape mode it degenerates into saturating, wobbling murk. The reverb gained a Reverse character whose impulse swells in instead of decaying, so every note arrives with a drawn-in breath.

Filter modes

Five types under Sound → Filter: Low, Band and High pass, Notch (the inverse of band-pass — it removes the band at the cut-off, sweeping a hollow through the sound), and Comb — a tuned feedback delay whose resonances sit at every multiple of the cut-off frequency. Comb with high resonance rings like a struck string and is superb for metallic drones; the envelope's → Filter amount sweeps its tuning.

Presets

The Presets panel saves the whole instrument: every control, the effect order, the FX matrix corners and cursor, armed motions, latches, and small copies of the three photos. Presets live only in this browser on this device — nothing is uploaded, and another device cannot read them. Export writes a preset to a single file (settings and photos included) that you can back up or move to another device yourself, and Import reads it back. Because browsers can reclaim site storage after long disuse, export the presets you care about.

Live camera

Live (under each photo in Edit) replaces that photo with the live camera. Latch a spot or arm a motion, then point the phone — whatever the camera sees plays the instrument, also while a MIDI file plays and the recorder runs. The camera image is only sampled on the device; nothing is sent anywhere.

The voice

The default analog engine is a virtual-analog monosynth running in an audio worklet: two band-limited (PolyBLEP) oscillators morph from sine through triangle to saw and pulse, drift a few cents apart the way two analogue oscillators never quite agree, and feed a zero-delay-feedback Moog ladder filter with a saturating core. Oscillators and filter run at twice the output rate and are filtered back down, so the waveshaping and the resonance stay clean instead of folding aliases back into the tone.

The additive engine is the alternative: ten pure sine partials and a biquad filter. Cleaner and glassier, and the fallback on browsers without audio worklets.

Colour → tone (photo 1)

  • Hue → pitch. With the default spectral law the hue is read as a wavelength (red ≈ 700 nm through to violet ≈ 380 nm) and the interval follows the photon energy E = hc/λ: semitones ∝ log(700 nm / λ). Red plays the base note, violet the top of the range you choose.
  • Saturation → waveform. A washed-out grey is close to a pure sine; as the colour saturates the wave morphs through triangle to a full saw or pulse.
  • Warm vs cool → odd/even balance. Reds fold in a square wave, which has only odd harmonics and sounds hollow and clarinet-like; blues stay on the saw and keep the complete harmonic series.
  • Green → detune and a formant bump on the third to fifth partial: more green, more beating between the two oscillators and more vocal edge.
  • Value → loudness. Dark corners of the photo are quiet, highlights are loud.

Colour → filter (photo 2)

  • Value → cut-off, from 80 Hz in the shadows to 14 kHz in the highlights, so a dark patch mutes the tone and a bright one opens it up.
  • Saturation → resonance, from nothing to the edge of self-oscillation.
  • Hue → a resonant peak swept from 180 Hz to 6 kHz by the same wavelength law, with its height set by saturation. Grey pixels leave the sound uncoloured.

The strip above the settings draws it: the bars are the harmonics of the current tone, the curve is the filter your second finger is describing, and the solid bars are what survives.

Keyboard and MIDI

Keys opens a keyboard that sets the base note — the note a pure red pixel plays. Everything the photo does is an interval above it, so the same photo can be played in any key. It follows the screen: fewer, wider keys in portrait, more of them in landscape, with the octave buttons to move. Hold keeps the last key down. On a computer the rows zsxdcvgbhnjm and q2w3er5t6y7u play the two octaves from the leftmost key.

A MIDI keyboard plays it the same way. Press Connect under File and the browser asks for MIDI access; from then on every key of the instrument sets the base note, so the photo still supplies the interval and the timbre. Notes light up on the on-screen keyboard, chords go to the poly slots like any other held key, the sustain pedal acts as Hold and the bend wheel bends ±2 semitones. Velocity decides how much a note's loudness follows how hard you play it — at 0 % every note is as loud as a touch on the photo. Pick a single Input and Channel if more than one device is talking, and Panic if a note ever hangs. Web MIDI exists in Chrome, Edge and Opera; Safari and Firefox do not have it, and there the button says so and nothing else changes.

A MIDI file plays the base note for you: import a .mid and its melody drives the transposition while the photos keep shaping the sound. The tempo comes from the file itself and the slider scales it from a quarter speed to four times. In Mono/Unison mode overlapping notes are reduced to the highest one; in Polyphonic mode the file's chords play for real, up to the Notes setting, both live and in the offline render.

Recording and export

Record captures the live output — playing, MIDI, filter sweeps and all — and Save WAV writes it out as 24-bit stereo at the output sample rate. Render MIDI → WAV instead renders the loaded file offline, faster than real time and sample-accurate, using the colours currently under the two cursors.

Photos

Camera opens the camera, Photos opens the camera roll (on iOS both live behind the same picker, so either button gets you there). Nothing is uploaded — the photos are decoded and read in the browser and never leave the phone. Demo loads a synthetic spectrum chart if you just want to try it.

On an iPhone the browser follows the ring/silent switch for web audio: if the pads look alive but you hear nothing, flip the switch or turn the volume up. Add the page to the home screen for a full-screen instrument.
